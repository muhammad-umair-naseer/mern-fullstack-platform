You can learn UI UX design for free if you learn it in the order the work actually happens: talk to users first, decide the structure second, style the screens third, and test with real people before you call anything finished. Most beginners do it backwards: they open Figma, copy a pretty app from Dribbble, and end up with screens that fall apart the moment someone uses them. Figma is free to start with, and the thinking costs nothing but practice.

This guide gives you an 8 week roadmap, the portfolio projects that prove you can do the job, the mistakes that make reviewers close a case study, and an honest look at our free [UI/UX Design Bootcamp](/learn/ui-ux-bootcamp), chapter by chapter.

## UI and UX are two halves of one job

People search for "UI UX" as one word because in most small teams, one person does both.

| | UX (user experience) | UI (user interface) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question it answers | Does this solve the right problem, in the right order? | Can people see, read and operate it? |
| Typical work | Interviews, personas, journey maps, task flows, wireframes, usability tests | Hierarchy, type scale, spacing, colour, components, prototypes |
| What goes wrong without it | A beautiful screen for a problem nobody has | A correct flow that nobody can read or tap |
| Main output | Evidence and structure | A design system a developer can build |

You need both. A UX designer who cannot make a readable screen gets ignored, and a UI designer who cannot explain why a screen exists gets overruled.

## What you need before you start

Less than you think.

- **A computer and a browser.** Figma runs in the browser and its free plan is enough for learning.
- **Paper and a pen.** Early sketches are faster on paper.
- **Three to five real people you can talk to.** You cannot practise interviewing alone.
- **No drawing talent.** UI work is boxes, type and spacing on a grid. Nobody hires you for your sketches.

## An 8 week roadmap to learn UI UX design for free

Plan for about an hour a day. Each week ends with something you can show.

| Week | Focus | What you finish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | User interviews and synthesis | Five interviews and a short list of insights with participant IDs |
| 2 | Personas and journey maps | One primary persona and a journey map showing where users drop off |
| 3 | Information architecture and task flows | A sitemap and flows that include error and empty states |
| 4 | Wireframes | Mid fidelity mobile wireframes with real text, no colour |
| 5 | Visual design | A type scale, a spacing scale and a colour palette that passes contrast checks |
| 6 | Figma components and prototype | Components built with auto layout, and a clickable prototype |
| 7 | Usability testing | Five test sessions and a list of fixes, ranked |
| 8 | Accessibility, handoff and case study | A revised design, developer notes and a written case study |

### Weeks 1 and 2: research first

Interview people about the last time they did the thing your product helps with, not about what they would like in future. "Would you use this?" gets a polite yes from everyone. "Tell me about the last time you tried to keep up with an online course" gets real details, including the workarounds people invent, which are the best evidence of a real need. Turn your notes into a persona built on behaviour, not age and hobbies, and a journey map that shows where people actually give up.

### Weeks 3 and 4: structure before pixels

Decide what goes where before you choose a single colour. Sketch a task flow for each main job, and draw the states nobody thinks about: the empty screen on first visit, the failed payment, the expired link. Then wireframe at mid fidelity, with real headings and real spacing but no colour, so reviewers argue about the flow instead of the button radius. Design the narrow mobile frame first, because squeezing a finished desktop layout down forces cuts nobody wants to make.

### Weeks 5 and 6: craft and system

Pick a type scale and a spacing scale and stick to them. Check every text and background pair against WCAG contrast; at the AA level, normal body text needs at least 4.5 to 1. In Figma, learn auto layout properly (it behaves much like CSS flexbox), build components with variants, and link a few screens into a prototype that is good enough to test.

### Weeks 7 and 8: prove it works

Give five people a task, stay quiet, and watch where they hesitate. Fix the worst problems, check keyboard and screen reader basics, write handoff notes, then write it all up as a case study.

## Portfolio projects that actually prove something

A reviewer wants to see your reasoning, not just your final screens. Pick a real problem close to you:

- **A local service redesign.** A clinic booking flow, a school fee portal, or a small shop's ordering page. Interview the people who use it.
- **A mobile app for a habit you know.** Studying, saving money, tracking a cricket team's practice. Keep the scope to one core task done very well.
- **A dashboard or data heavy screen.** Harder, and rarer in junior portfolios.

For each one, show the problem, what you learned from users, the options you rejected and why, the test results, and what you changed. One honest case study beats five Dribbble copies.

## Mistakes that slow beginners down

1. **Starting in high fidelity.** Polished screens hide bad flows.
2. **Asking users what they want.** Ask what they did last time instead.
3. **Designing only the happy path.** Developers will improvise the missing error screens, usually as a raw error message.
4. **Using a hamburger menu for everything.** Hidden navigation gets used less. On mobile, three to five clear tabs usually serve people better.
5. **Light grey text on white.** It looks elegant and fails contrast.
6. **Skipping testing because you are sure.** Everyone is sure until the first session.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Can I really learn UI UX design for free?

Yes. The course, quizzes and certificate are free, and Figma's free plan is enough to practise. What you cannot skip is time with real users.

### How long does it take to learn UI UX design?

About 8 weeks at an hour a day gets you through the core skills and one solid case study. Becoming job ready usually takes a few more projects and honest feedback on them.

### Do I need to know coding to become a UI UX designer?

No. It helps to understand how layouts behave in a browser, which is why the course explains Figma auto layout in terms of CSS, but you can design well without writing code.

### Is UI UX design hard to learn?

The tools are quick to pick up. The hard part is the habits: asking good questions, designing every state, and accepting what testing tells you.

### What should I put in my first UI UX portfolio?

Two or three case studies on real problems, each showing research, rejected options, test results and what you changed. Process matters more than polish.
