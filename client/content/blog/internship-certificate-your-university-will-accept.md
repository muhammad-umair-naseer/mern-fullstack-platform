Most departments want four things, not one: a certificate on company letterhead stating your role and the exact start and end dates, a signed recommendation or completion letter from your supervisor, whatever evaluation proforma the department itself hands out, and some way for a coordinator to confirm the company is real and that you were actually there.

The mistake almost everyone makes is asking for all of this on the last day. By then your supervisor has moved on, the person who prints letterhead is on leave, and nobody remembers your exact joining date. Ask in week one.

## What your department is really checking

Coordinators are not reading your certificate for the compliments. Under guidelines from the [Higher Education Commission (HEC) Pakistan](https://www.hec.gov.pk/) and computing accreditation bodies like [NCEAC](https://nceac.org.pk/), they are answering three separate verification questions:

1. Is this organisation real and legally registered?
2. Did this student actually work there, for the period claimed?
3. Was the work related to the degree?

Each one is satisfied by a different piece of paper, which is why a single certificate that says "has successfully completed an internship at our esteemed organisation" usually gets sent back. It answers none of the three.

## The documents, and who produces each

| Document | Who issues it | When to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Offer or joining letter | The company | Before you start |
| Internship certificate | The company, on letterhead, signed and stamped | Final week |
| Supervisor recommendation letter | Your named supervisor, on letterhead | Final week |
| Department evaluation proforma | Your department gives you the blank form, your supervisor fills and signs it | Week one |
| Internship report | You write it | Throughout |
| Attendance or timesheet record | You keep it, supervisor countersigns | Weekly |

The proforma is the one that ruins people. It is your department's own form, it varies between universities and sometimes between departments in the same university, and no company can guess what is on it. Collect the blank form before you start and hand it over in your first week. A supervisor will happily fill a form in front of them. Chasing one by email in December, after the internship ended in August, is a different experience.

## What has to be on the certificate itself

If any of these are missing, expect a question:

- Your full name exactly as it appears on your CNIC and university record. "Ali Raza" on the certificate and "Syed Ali Raza" on the enrolment form creates work for you.
- Your role or track. "Frontend Development Intern", not "Intern".
- Exact calendar start and end dates. Not "one month". Not "summer 2026".
- Total duration stated in weeks or months, matching those dates.
- One or two lines on what you actually worked on.
- Letterhead carrying the registered company name, physical address, phone number and an email address, ideally on the company's own domain.
- The signatory's printed name and designation, not just a signature.
- Company stamp and date of issue.
- A reference or serial number, if the company uses them.

The most common reason a certificate looks suspicious to a coordinator is not fraud. It is the combination: a free webmail address as the only contact, an unnamed signature, and no dates. Plenty of real companies run on a Gmail address, ours included, so it is the other two that do most of the damage. Perfectly genuine internships get flagged for exactly this.

## Why verification suddenly matters

Forging a certificate takes a template and twenty minutes, and everyone involved knows it. So departments stopped treating the paper as proof and started treating it as a claim to be checked. What we see them actually do:

- Phone the number printed on the letterhead and ask whether the student worked there.
- Email the supervisor at the company domain and expect a reply from that domain. Some coordinators will not accept a reply from a free webmail account.
- Look up the company registration. In Pakistan that usually means an SECP company search, or asking for the registration number or NTN.

## Duration and dates, where most certificates fail

Universities tend to specify a duration in weeks. Six and eight weeks are the common ones. Companies tend to run internships in months. These do not line up, and the gap causes most of the certificate disputes we hear about.

Three rules:

**Check the requirement before you apply, not after.** Your handbook or coordinator will state the minimum duration and often whether it must be continuous. Take that number into the conversation with the company.

**Get the dates written down before you start.** An offer letter with a start and end date on it means the certificate at the end has nothing to negotiate.

**Never ask anyone to stretch the dates.** This is the part worth being blunt about. If you did four weeks and the requirement is six, asking for a certificate that says six turns a scheduling problem into a disciplinary one. When a coordinator phones and the supervisor answers honestly, and supervisors do answer honestly, you are no longer arguing about a shortfall. You are arguing about a forged document. Any company that agrees to inflate the dates for you has just told you exactly how much its certificate is worth to anyone who checks.

If you are genuinely short, say so and ask whether the internship can be extended, or whether a second block later in the year can be certified separately. Two honest certificates are accepted far more often than people expect.

One related detail: if your requirement is full time and you interned part time or remotely, ask for the certificate to state the working pattern. Departments differ on whether that is acceptable, and it is much better to find out in week one.

## Ask for this in your first week

Send your supervisor one short email at the start, not at the end:

```
Subject: Internship documentation for university submission

Hello [supervisor],

My university requires the following for my internship credit. Sharing
it now so nothing is last minute:

1. Joining or offer letter confirming my role and start date
2. On completion: a certificate on company letterhead with exact start
   and end dates, duration, my role, and a company stamp
3. On completion: a recommendation letter from you, with your
   designation and official email address
4. My department's evaluation form, attached, to be filled and signed
   at the end
5. Confirmation of who my department should contact if they need to
   verify the internship

My department's minimum duration is [X] weeks and my submission
deadline is [date].

Thank you,
[name], [roll number], [department], [university]
```

Nothing in that email is unreasonable, and a company that runs internships properly will have answers ready. A company that goes quiet on point five has told you something useful, early.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What happens if my university requires a 6-week internship but the company offers 4 weeks?
Speak with your company supervisor in week one. Most software houses allow students to extend their duration by two weeks or split the requirement across two blocks. Never request artificial date alterations on the final letter.

### Do Pakistani universities accept certificates from remote internships?
Most major universities (including NUST, FAST, COMSATS, and Bahria) accept remote or hybrid software internships provided the certificate clearly outlines your project deliverables, timesheets, and supervisor contact details.

### How do university coordinators verify that an internship is authentic?
University department heads or placement officers typically call the official company landline/phone number printed on the letterhead, email the supervisor at their corporate domain, or check the company's registration on the SECP/FBR database.

### Can an online course completion certificate replace a mandatory university internship?
No. An online course certificate verifies self-paced study and testing, whereas university degree accreditation bodies (like HEC and NCEAC) strictly mandate supervised workplace training under an industry mentor.
